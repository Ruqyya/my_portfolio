# 01 · Competitive research: 10 healthcare product designers

Researched 23 Oct 2026.

## How this research was done (read first)

- **Perplexity was not used.** The only Perplexity connector available in this session ("Perplexity Computer") can only receive Claude dashboards. It has no search tool. Research was done with web search and by opening each portfolio directly (page text via fetch, and the rendered page in a browser for the two sites whose text did not load).
- **What I could see:** each site's written content, structure and numbers. **What I could not see:** colour, typography, spacing and motion. The text capture does not include them, so those columns are marked "not assessed" rather than guessed. A screenshot was only taken for pr4veen.in.
- **Seniority and healthcare experience are as each designer states them on their own site.** I did not find independent confirmation (employer pages, press) for any of them.
- **Two candidates are weak healthcare evidence** (Hicks, Inyang). I could not find verified stronger replacements with the search tools available, so I kept them and flagged them. Treat the list as 8 strong healthcare examples and 2 pattern references.
- Nothing here is copied. Only patterns are recorded.

## Per-designer findings (observed, then interpretation)

### 1 · Matt Schoolfield
- **Observed:** Headline about "systems" and "AI-native design"; two CTAs; three scale stats (1M+ patients, 70+ countries, 400+ clinical trials). Seven case cards with number, category tag, summary. Outcomes quoted: "~$100M in potential new sponsor revenue", "estimated $18M", "GMV up 10% at launch". Four-pillar approach section.
- **Interpretation:** Strong healthcare credibility through scale. Several figures are hedged estimates with no method. The hero is abstract.
- **Lesson:** Lead with real scale, but say how a number was measured.

### 2 · Aaron Engel
- **Observed:** "A hands-on designer who also works through the strategy, the technical detail, and the alignment across teams." Cards carry a context tag ("Fortune 100 · Program leadership") and role. Outcomes: one model across six teams and 50+ people serving 1M+ users; COCA meets WCAG 2.1 AA. Consulting client logos.
- **Interpretation:** Clear, honest scope labels. Fittra card has no results. A COCA thumbnail appears to lack alt text, which undercuts an accessibility claim.
- **Lesson:** Put role and context on every card. Practise the accessibility you claim.

### 3 · Steve Libbey
- **Observed:** Title "Senior Product Designer for Healthcare, Analytics, and Complex Systems"; "20+ years"; clients Microsoft, T-Mobile, Boeing, Providence Healthcare. Six selected case studies, each a one-sentence problem-and-action summary. Closing section states what roles he is seeking.
- **Interpretation:** Very precise domain statement. No numeric outcomes on the home page.
- **Lesson:** Name your domains precisely and say what you want next.

### 4 · Anna Szumska
- **Observed:** "Designing for regulated complexity. Built for humans."; stats (5+ years, 4 industries, 21+ products); six cards with role, dates and tags such as "WCAG 2.1 AA"; one case study is password-protected; CV download; copy-email confirmation. Metrics: 35% efficiency gain, 1,200+ users.
- **Interpretation:** Excellent positioning and scannable cards. The locked case study and unexplained 35% weaken proof. AI emphasis is heavy.
- **Lesson:** Regulated-industry framing works. Do not lock your best evidence.

### 5 · Nick Clement (Numan case study only)
- **Observed:** Headline "Not a pharmacy. A clinic that stays." Metadata block (role, studio, sector). Sections: Overview, Challenge, Approach, Outcome, then previous/next navigation. Figures shown are company-level (funding, ARR, patients), not design results.
- **Interpretation:** A memorable one-line framing and a clean structure. The text capture shows no process artefacts or screens.
- **Lesson:** A strong reframing line makes a case study memorable. Separate company results from your contribution.

### 6 · Catherine Hicks
- **Observed:** "Product Designer · AI-in-the-loop", 20+ years. Portfolio organised into eight categories with project counts. No outcomes shown. Healthcare category lists six projects, one previewed.
- **Interpretation:** Easy to scan by category, but no evidence of impact, and healthcare is a small part.
- **Lesson:** Category sorting helps; it does not replace results.

### 7 · Leon Barnard
- **Observed:** "Solving Thorny UX Problems for 20 Years." Featured case study structure: Problem, Business Impact, five-step Process, Result, Impact, "Wins and Wishes". Before/after screenshots. No quantified results ("Customers were pleased"). Testimonials unattributed; several typos and mislabelled files.
- **Interpretation:** The candid "Wishes" section reads as senior honesty. Missing numbers and sloppy details hurt credibility.
- **Lesson:** Admit what you would do differently, and proofread everything.

### 8 · Priyanka Kumari
- **Observed:** "11+ years"; Oracle NetSuite and HonorHealth. Each card shows metrics: +34% booking completion (HonorHealth, 18 months), $15M added revenue, 150K+ hours saved. A large art section. The tagline text is garbled ("simplifiessimplifiesdesignsships"); the timeline starts at item 3.
- **Interpretation:** Metrics on every card is the right instinct; no baselines or methods are shown. Visible mistakes on the first screen cost trust.
- **Lesson:** Put the metric on the card, with context. Check the first screen obsessively.

### 9 · Kubiat Inyang
- **Observed:** Text-led hero: "Some designers avoid the hard stuff. I've made it my specialty." Four projects with scope lists; design system quantified (200+ tokens, 70+ components, 20+ templates). States case studies are "in progress".
- **Interpretation:** Distinctive voice and honesty. No visuals, and healthcare is a single tag.
- **Lesson:** A confident voice is memorable, but visual evidence is not optional.

### 10 · Praveen Kumar N
- **Observed (rendered page):** "I design complex products that feel simple." ~9 years in product design; "3 → 1 products unified into one platform"; "40% adoption lift, Spot Doctor app"; "135 design-system components"; a personal "Why design" quote. A floating icon dock for navigation.
- **Interpretation:** The best match for outcome-led senior positioning: four facts a hiring manager can repeat. Context for how 40% was measured is not on the home page.
- **Lesson:** Four hard facts in the hero beat a paragraph.

## Scoring rubric (qualitative, transparent)

For each criterion I assign one of four codes, based only on what was visible:

- **Healthcare evidence:** none / one tag / one project / several named projects or clients.
- **Impact:** none / qualitative only / numbers without method / numbers with context.
- **Case study depth:** summary only / problem+result / full structure with artefacts / not visible.
- **Honesty signals:** none / some limits stated / explicit reflection on gaps.

Results: strongest healthcare evidence is Schoolfield, Libbey, Engel, Szumska, Kumar N. Impact with numbers: Schoolfield, Szumska, Kumari, Kumar N (none give a measurement method). Explicit reflection on gaps: Barnard, Inyang.

## Five patterns strong portfolios share
1. A specific domain statement in the title or headline (Libbey, Szumska, Schoolfield, Kumar N).
2. Hard numbers near the top (Schoolfield, Szumska, Kumar N, Kumari).
3. Project cards that carry role, context and tags (Engel, Szumska).
4. A clear problem-to-result path in the case study (Barnard, Clement).
5. An obvious way to get in touch or download a CV (Szumska, Libbey).

## Five patterns to avoid
1. Using "AI" as the headline crutch (four of the ten lead with it).
2. Identical tag-title-one-liner card grids.
3. Percentages with no baseline or method.
4. The dark background + oversized headline + stat row combination.
5. Locked or thin case studies that stop at a teaser.

## Five ways to differentiate
1. Show one real product across *all* its users (patient, clinician, front desk) as a navigable figure.
2. Video-led project pages (none of the ten captured pages described film; this is not proof they have none).
3. State scope honestly ("what this film shows") and never invent a number.
4. A warm editorial palette instead of dark or blue SaaS.
5. Make the site itself pass an accessibility audit, since several competitors claim accessibility.

## What reads as senior
Scale of users or sites; ownership scope (who the designer led, which systems unified); strategic framing ("not a pharmacy, a clinic that stays"); candid trade-offs and reflection; evidence of working across research, design and engineering.

## What attracts healthcare design recruiters
Job listings found in search ask for: 3–5 detailed case studies with problem, role, process, and measurable outcomes; EHR/clinical workflow familiarity; accessibility (WCAG) and compliance awareness; shipped work with post-launch iteration; cross-functional work with clinicians and engineers. These are employer wishes from one job aggregator, not an industry standard.
